Unprecedented Chiba flooding kills six, triggers mass evacuations and Narita crisis

At least six people died and nearly 7,000 were stranded at Narita airport after 115mm of rain fell in a single hour on Chiba Prefecture on August 14, the Japan Meteorological Agency issuing its Level 5 Emergency Warning — its highest threshold. Evacuation orders were extended to more than 400,000 residents and approximately 45,000 homes lost power, with landslide warnings active across the region.

So what: The Chiba flooding adds a second simultaneous domestic crisis to the Kumamoto earthquake recovery already consuming the Takaichi Cabinet's political bandwidth, compressing bandwidth ahead of the August 15 WWII anniversary.

More Than Entertainment: Japan's Creative Industries and the U.S.-Japan Partnership
Japan's creative industries — anime, gaming, music, and content exports — constitute an underappreciated but strategically meaningful dimension of the US-Japan partnership that merits explicit policy attention.
The CSIS Japan Chair piece argues that Japan's creative industries represent more than soft power or commercial entertainment, framing them as a structural pillar of the bilateral partnership with implications for cultural diplomacy, economic cooperation, and people-to-people ties. The analysis situates creative-sector collaboration within the broader US-Japan strategic relationship, suggesting policy frameworks should formally incorporate cultural industries alongside the alliance's security and economic pillars.
As the US-Japan alliance increasingly encompasses economic security and technology cooperation, institutionalizing creative-industry collaboration could provide a durable, politically resilient dimension of bilateral engagement that supplements defense and trade frameworks.
Japan Economy Watch (Katz)
Myth: US Intervened to Support Yen Out Of Fears About US Interest Rates
US yen-support intervention was not driven by concerns about US interest rates, contra the dominant market narrative.
Japan Economy Watch challenges the prevailing interpretation that US participation in yen-support operations was motivated by domestic US rate concerns, arguing the intervention rationale is rooted in exchange-rate and alliance-coordination considerations rather than Federal Reserve policy anxiety. The piece is directly relevant as carry traders actively exploit intervention windows to rebuild yen shorts and the BOJ-Treasury dynamic around the yen remains a live policy tension.
The distinction matters for forecasting: if US intervention rationale is alliance-based rather than rate-driven, future yen-support coordination is more predictable — and less dependent on Fed policy shifts — than markets currently assume.
