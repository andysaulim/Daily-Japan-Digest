Council on Foreign Relations
Why the U.S. Intervened to Prop Up Japan's Yen
The US decision to intervene in currency markets to support the yen reflects strategic calculations about the dollar's global role and the alliance relationship, not just bilateral trade grievances.
The CFR piece examines Washington's motivations for joining Japan's yen stabilization operation, situating the intervention within both alliance management and broader dollar-dominance concerns. It addresses the political economy of why a Trump administration would take an action — currency market intervention to weaken the dollar — that runs against standard Republican economic orthodoxy. The piece argues the 'dollar boomerang' risk — where a weak yen destabilizes Asian financial markets in ways that ultimately feed back into US financial conditions — was a core motivator.
The CFR framing suggests the US yen intervention sets a precedent for treating allied-currency stabilization as a legitimate national-security tool, with implications for future trade negotiation leverage and host-nation support discussions.
Council on Foreign Relations
The Dollar Boomerang Threat: Washington's Motivations to Support the Yen
Washington intervened to support the yen because a sustained weak yen risks destabilizing Asian currency markets in ways that ultimately harm US financial interests — the 'dollar boomerang' dynamic.
This CFR piece provides an analytical framework for the US motivation in joining Japan's yen intervention, centering on the risk that competitive depreciation across Asia in response to yen weakness would ultimately force a destabilizing dollar adjustment. It ties the intervention rationale to broader US concerns about reserve-currency management and Asian financial stability. The piece contextualizes Bessent's public statements about intervening 'for stability of Asia' within this framework.
The 'dollar boomerang' argument, if it represents the actual US policy rationale, means the US calculus for future yen-support actions will track Asian currency contagion risk rather than bilateral trade balances.
