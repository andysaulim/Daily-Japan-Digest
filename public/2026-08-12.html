DPRK

North Korea fires second ballistic missile in six days; falls west of Hokkaido outside Japan's EEZ

North Korea launched a ballistic missile toward the Sea of Japan on August 12, reaching approximately 56 miles altitude before falling west of Hokkaido outside Japan's EEZ — its second launch in less than a week. Japan's Defense Ministry confirmed the launch; Japan lodged a formal protest, and South Korea, the US, and Japan jointly pressed North Korea to halt launches. South China Morning Post and NK News reporting notes the launch timing — ahead of US-ROK Ulchi Freedom Shield exercises — and that the missile profile hints at upgraded weapons systems potentially linked to exports to Russia.

So what: The back-to-back launch cadence, timed ahead of Ulchi Freedom Shield, sets the provocation baseline for the exercise period and will drive trilateral US-Japan-ROK coordination decisions in the coming days.

Pattern: Prior years have seen DPRK launch activity coincide with or immediately precede US-ROK exercise periods; the August 7 launch — North Korea's first ballistic launch since April — and this August 12 launch represent the fastest back-to-back sequence in the current series.

Economy-BOJ

Bessent-Takaichi split on BOJ rate path threatens durability of coordinated yen intervention

US Treasury Secretary Bessent believes BOJ tightening is essential to address yen weakness, while PM Takaichi has long resisted taking rates too high — a divide that Japan Times and Bloomberg report threatens to undermine the joint yen rescue. Multiple sources identify BOJ Governor Ueda's early September rate-hike signal as the triggering condition for what is described as the first coordinated US-Japan yen intervention in 28 years; the yen has since surrendered roughly half its intervention gains.

So what: The BOJ's September 17-18 Monetary Policy Meeting becomes the near-term test: a rate hike would validate Bessent's position but conflict with Takaichi's fiscal preferences, while a hold could reignite yen selling pressure.

Pattern: Japan Economy Watch and Bloomberg both note the yen has already given back approximately half of its intervention gains, a pattern consistent with prior unilateral yen-defense episodes when underlying policy divergence was not resolved.

BOJ rate-hike path runs into Takaichi's bond market constraints

Reuters reports that the BOJ's projected rate-hike trajectory is colliding with structural bond-market pressures linked to PM Takaichi's fiscal stance, including her commitment to a food consumption tax cut that markets view as adding to Japan's debt load. The Japan Times separately notes that fiscal questions — particularly Takaichi's grocery tax cut pledge — are weighing on market confidence that intervention and rate hikes alone will anchor the yen.

So what: The intersection of BOJ normalization, Takaichi's fiscal expansion, and yen instability will shape the Diet's fall budget deliberations and the trajectory of Japan's JGB yields.

Yen Has Already Given Up Half Of Its Gains From Joint US-Japan Intervention
The yen has already surrendered approximately half of its gains from the recent US-Japan coordinated intervention, suggesting the intervention's stabilizing effect is eroding without durable policy alignment.
Japan Economy Watch argues that the yen's rapid partial reversal after the coordinated US-Japan intervention exposes the structural weakness in using FX intervention without resolving the underlying BOJ-fiscal policy divergence. The piece situates the intervention's partial failure within the broader Bessent-Takaichi split on optimal BOJ rate policy. The analysis implies that without a September BOJ rate hike or a shift in Takaichi's fiscal stance, another intervention round may be needed soon.
The yen's erosion of intervention gains sets up the BOJ's September 17-18 meeting as the primary near-term test of whether the joint rescue can be sustained.
Asia Policy Point
Gaming the Consumption Tax Cut in Japan
Japan's planned consumption tax cut on food carries significant fiscal and distributional risks that the Takaichi government has not fully accounted for in its current framing.
Asia Policy Point examines the political economy of Takaichi's two-year food consumption tax cut to 1% from April 2027, arguing the measure creates gaming opportunities in supply chains and retail pricing that could offset its intended relief for consumers. The piece connects the tax cut's fiscal cost to the broader bond market pressure flagged by Reuters and the Japan Times today. The analysis raises questions about whether the cut will translate into consumer price relief or be captured by intermediaries.
The Diet's fall budget session will need to reconcile the consumption tax cut's fiscal cost against rising JGB yields and the BOJ's rate normalization path.
